A precision upgrade should be judged by how many tasks it saves minus how many it spoils. This signed objective is distinct from agreement with BF16 and from the number of changed outcomes. The reference experiments make that distinction testable.

\paragraph{Match the label to the executed mode.} The three upgrade modes spend BF16 computation at different points. Replacing one action lets Q handle its consequences. Taking over the continuation preserves Q's current action. Joint replacement changes both. A selector's outcome target should match the mode it will execute. A label for joint takeover can count a success that action replacement cannot obtain. Conversely, a success under Q's continuation can disappear under B's. These patterns make the evaluation target consequential even when average gains are similar. BF16 also need not be the better end-to-end policy: Qwen3-4B GPTQ-W4 achieves 40.0\% end-to-end DB success versus BF16's 35.8\% (Appendix~\ref{app:e2e}).

\paragraph{Optimise benefit, not response to intervention.} Our frozen margin rule finds outcome changes on later blocks of the same tasks. It captures rescues and damages together, giving no reliable net benefit over matched random selection. The finding concerns this frozen rule. The hindsight oracle identifies available gains after outcomes are known; it does not establish that Q-visible features can predict them. Learned routing should be evaluated on held-out decisions by signed task benefit. Cost-aware comparisons should also match BF16 computation, because continuation takeover can use more decisions than a single-action replacement.

Turnover gives a rescue and a damage the same positive weight; net benefit gives them opposite signs. A rule can become better at finding sensitive decisions while remaining unhelpful for task success. Its upgrades must save more tasks than they spoil, relative to a policy spending the same fallback budget.

\paragraph{From realised consequences to deployment value.} Exact replay makes a comparison auditable under fixed execution conditions. It does not make that setting representative of deployment. Key-order reversal demonstrates that individual labels can depend on the representation the agent reads. To estimate expected benefit, specify a distribution of prompts and user conditions, repeat paired branches across it, and average the success differences. Average benefit and individual label stability are separate questions. Our task-level intervals address task sampling; they do not cover every change to the user model or serving stack. On the telecom holdout, the action-only estimate indicates a net loss in the tested setting, although the frozen protocol has no category for it. Because domain and tasks change together, these data do not identify the cause or establish a general domain-transfer effect.

\paragraph{What the references change.} A turnover statistic combines beneficial and harmful interventions. It cannot say which side dominates, or whether the change required more bits. The same-precision checkpoint answers the latter question by supplying a different action without changing bit width. Key reordering goes further: the executed tool operation stays fixed, yet the later outcome can change. These observations limit an attribution from a flip to a precision deficit. They do not establish that BF16 has no additional value. That stronger question requires the shared-state value comparison, whose uncertainty remains substantial here.

\paragraph{A concrete evaluation target.} A router trained to predict disagreement can succeed on that target while failing to save tasks. Our trigger experiment evaluates both quantities on the same selected states, so enrichment cannot substitute for the benefit test. For an action-only router, the desired target is the task success difference after Q handles the substituted action. For a takeover router, it is the difference after B handles the continuation. A task-saving policy must avoid damaging successes that Q would have retained, so evaluation must count both directions.

\paragraph{Coverage and practical use.} Table~\ref{tab:scope} extends the measurement to BFCL and ALFWorld, which have no LLM user, and to Mistral, a second model family. The selected RTN-W3 example shows that a degraded agent can benefit from takeover even when action-only replacement is unhelpful. Each upgrade needs a signed-benefit test on its intended states. Appendix~\ref{app:scope} gives the paired estimates and controls.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Precision fallback should be evaluated by the task benefit of the upgrade actually executed. Our audited forks distinguish action replacement, continuation takeover and joint replacement. The W4 panel shows substantial rescues and damages with little net gain. Same-precision and representation references establish why flips alone cannot diagnose a need for more bits. A useful selector must identify signed benefit under the intended continuation.
